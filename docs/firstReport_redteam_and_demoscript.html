FirstReport red team attack with fixes, and a 2-minute demo script

A
Red team — 5 reasons this will not win
KILL SHOT 1

"This is a legal advice tool. You will be disqualified or warned for liability."

Judges at Google DeepMind are not lawyers, but their legal team is. A tool that tells a user "your offense is cognizable, police refusal was unlawful" and then generates a court petition is giving legal advice. One wrong classification — calling a non-cognizable offense cognizable — and a victim submits a false complaint to an SP, potentially exposing themselves to counter-action under S.182 BNSS (false information to public servant). The judges will flag this. They will ask: did you get legal review? The answer is no. Submissions with unmitigated harm risk score down regardless of technical quality.

Concrete fix

Reframe the tool as a "legal literacy and document drafting assistant" — not a classifier that tells users what the law says about their case. Concretely: rename the classification screen from "Police inkaar gairkanooni tha" (Police refusal was unlawful) to "Kanoon kehta hai: [offense type] ke liye FIR likhna zaroori hota hai" (The law says: for [offense type], FIR registration is mandatory). Shift from verdict to statute quotation. Add a mandatory screen before document generation: "Yeh document ek draft hai. Submit karne se pehle NALSA helpline 15100 se baat karein." Make the NALSA number a tap-to-call button. This converts the tool from legal advisor to informed citizen assistant — a framing that survives legal review and is still the most powerful tool a victim has ever seen.

KILL SHOT 2

"You haven't shown a single real user validating this. It's built on assumptions."

Judges who have seen 600 submissions know the pattern: builder in Bangalore builds for a user in Ghaziabad they have never spoken to, assuming the user will adopt the behaviour the tool requires. You are assuming Sunita will photograph a refusal slip she may not have received, speak coherently into a phone immediately after a traumatic police interaction, confirm a legal classification she cannot verify, and submit a formal document to an SP she has never written to. Zero field validation. Competing teams from NGOs and social enterprises will have pilot data. You will not.

Concrete fix

Before submission day, contact two organisations by email: HRLN (Human Rights Law Network) and iCall (TISS). Send them a 3-minute video of the tool working. Ask one question: "Does this match how victims you work with would actually use a phone after a police refusal?" One reply, even a one-line "yes, this addresses a real gap we see," is a validation quote you put on your submission page. If you get nothing in time: screenshot 5 posts from r/legaladviceindia or localcircles.com where users describe FIR refusal — these are real users describing the exact problem. Quote them verbatim in your submission. Evidence of real user pain beats a clean UI slide every time.

KILL SHOT 3

"Gemma 4 is doing nothing here that GPT-3.5 with a lookup table couldn't do in 2022."

A skeptical judge will look at your pipeline and see: STT → keyword extraction → SQLite lookup → template fill → PDF. The "Gemma 4" label is on top of what is fundamentally a rule-based system with a language model for text generation. The offense classification is the only step where a model is genuinely necessary — but you haven't proven that Gemma 4 specifically is needed for it, or that a fine-tuned smaller model wouldn't do it better. The multimodal capability of Gemma 4 (its key differentiator over all predecessors) is used only for refusal slip OCR — a task that Google Vision API has done since 2016. The judge will write: "Gemma 4's unique capabilities underutilized."

Concrete fix

Add one demo moment that is genuinely multimodal and impossible without Gemma 4's vision-language fusion. Specifically: victim photographs the police station notice board (every station has one listing the duty officer's name, batch number, and current posting). Gemma 4 vision reads the board, extracts the officer's name and designation, and auto-populates the SP complaint with "Against: Inspector [Name], Batch No. [X], currently posted at [Station]" — information the victim would otherwise not know how to formally record. This is not OCR (notice boards are cluttered, handwritten, partially obscured) — it requires vision-language reasoning to extract structured fields from an unstructured scene. Now Gemma 4's multimodal capability is load-bearing, not decorative. Show this moment explicitly in the demo.

KILL SHOT 4

"The demo will break on the one input you didn't prepare for."

You have rehearsed theft and domestic violence. A judge will say "my phone was snatched" (robbery vs theft — different BNSS sections, different cognizability nuance), or "landlord locked me out" (wrongful restraint — S.126 BNSS, cognizable), or "auto driver overcharged and abused me" (cheating + criminal intimidation — two sections, both cognizable). Any scenario with ambiguity, multiple offenses, or a Hindi description that uses regional vocabulary ("dhamki di" vs "maar-peet ki" vs "rokke rakha") risks a wrong classification, a visible model hesitation, or a crash. Judges specifically choose adversarial inputs for live demos. One stumble destroys the credibility of the legal citation accuracy that the entire product depends on.

Concrete fix

Build a hard-coded fallback for ambiguous multi-offense inputs. If Gemma's classification output contains more than one BNSS section, or if the narrative contains two distinct incident types, route to a screen that says: "Aapke case mein ek se zyada dhara lag sakti hai. Sabse serious dhara chunein:" and shows a two-button choice between the classified options, each with a one-line plain Hindi description. The victim chooses; the document generates from their choice. This converts a model failure mode into a product feature — "FirstReport handles complex cases by letting the victim confirm the most serious offense." Rehearse this path explicitly with the phone-snatch, landlord-lockout, and multi-offense scenarios until it works on all three.

KILL SHOT 5

"Impact is theoretical. You have no evidence the documents actually work."

Your submission will claim "30 million unregistered crimes" and "empowers victims." A judge will ask: has anyone ever submitted one of your generated documents and gotten an FIR registered as a result? The answer is no — the tool doesn't exist yet. Worse: if the document has a formatting error, a wrong section citation, or a tone that a bureaucrat rejects on sight, it may actively harm the victim by burning their credibility before they can escalate properly. You are making an implicit claim of efficacy for a legal instrument you have never tested in the real world. Competing health submissions will show pilot data, clinical validation, or at minimum expert endorsement. You have none.

Concrete fix

Get one practising advocate to review one generated document and give you a written quote. Email five lawyers through Bar Council referrals or LawRato's advocate directory with the subject line "Pro bono review of legal aid AI tool — 20 minutes." Send them one generated SP complaint PDF. Ask: "Is this document legally sound? Would an SP accept it?" One response saying "the format and citations are correct" is worth more than any technical diagram in your submission. If you get zero responses in time: go to a legal aid clinic (every district court has one) in person, show them the document on your phone, and ask the same question. Write down what they say verbatim and include it as a quote. This converts "theoretical impact" into "expert-reviewed output" — a qualitatively different claim.

B
2-minute demo script — spoken, tight
0–10s
Hook
[Show: a real handwritten sign on a UP police station wall — "FIR nahi likhi jayegi." Or a WhatsApp screenshot: "police ne bhaga diya."]

"This is a police station in Ghaziabad. A woman came here after her savings were stolen. The officer said: 'civil matter, go home.' She left. She didn't know he had just broken the law."
10–50s
Live system
[Screen: Streamlit app, airplane mode indicator visible top-right. No internet. No API calls.]

"This is FirstReport. No internet. Watch."

[Press mic. Speak in Hindi:] "Padosi ne almari se paisa churaya. Thane ne FIR likhne se mana kar diya."

[Pause 5 seconds — tokens stream on screen]

"Gemma 4 — running on this laptop, offline — has just read that Hindi sentence, matched it to Section 303 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, and confirmed the refusal was illegal."

[Classification screen appears with BNSS citation. Tap confirm. Documents begin generating.]

"Three legal documents. Forty-two seconds. The SP complaint is already addressed to the Superintendent of Police, Ghaziabad — name, address, WhatsApp number — pulled from a local database. No hallucination. No internet."
50–90s
Impact
[Show: the three document cards on screen — SP ready, DM locked with 3-day countdown, HC locked with 18-day countdown]

"She doesn't submit one letter and hope. She has a three-rung escalation ladder — legally timed — already prepared. If the SP ignores her, the District Magistrate complaint unlocks automatically in three days. If the DM ignores her, the High Court writ is ready on day eighteen."

"30 million cognizable offenses go unregistered in India every year. The law already gave every one of those victims the right to fight back. Nobody told them how. The BNSS replaced the CrPC in July 2024 — every legal aid app in India still cites the wrong law. FirstReport is the only tool built on the new code."
90–120s
Wow moment
[New input — photograph of a police station notice board, officer name partially visible]

"One more thing. She photographed the notice board on her way out."

[Gemma vision reads it. Officer name and batch number auto-populate into the SP complaint.]

"The officer who turned her away is now named — by batch number, by posting — in a formal complaint addressed to his superior, citing the specific section of Indian law that makes his refusal a criminal act."

"She walked in powerless. She left with a case file."

"The law was always on her side. She just needed a phone."

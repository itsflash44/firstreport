FirstReport — complete hackathon submission plan for the FIR refusal BNSS escalation tool

1
Problem story

"Sunita walked two kilometres to the Govindpuram police station after her neighbour stole ₹40,000 of her husband's savings from a box under the bed. The duty officer told her it was a 'family matter' and sent her home. She didn't know that what she witnessed was Section 303 BNSS — theft from a dwelling — a cognizable offense the officer was legally required to register. She didn't know that refusing to register was itself a crime under Section 166 BNSS. She didn't know there was a Superintendent of Police, a District Magistrate, and a High Court she could escalate to — in that order — each with a statutory deadline. She went home. The money was never recovered."

India's NCRB data shows 10.5 million cognizable offenses registered in 2022 — researchers consistently estimate 30–40 million go unregistered, with police refusal as a leading cause. The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), which replaced the CrPC on July 1 2024, strengthened the victim's escalation rights under Sections 173, 175(3), and 166. Every major legal aid app in India still cites the old CrPC section numbers. Not a single tool exists that walks a victim through the new BNSS escalation chain — offline, in Hindi, in three minutes.

3
Why current solutions fail
Failure 1 — Every existing legal aid tool runs on CrPC, which was repealed in July 2024

NALSA's app, Nyaya Mitra, iCanReach, and all state legal aid apps cite Section 154 CrPC and Section 156(3) CrPC for FIR complaints and magistrate petitions. Those sections no longer exist in Indian law. The correct citations are Section 173 BNSS (FIR obligation), Section 166 BNSS (officer failure penalty), and Section 175(3) BNSS (magistrate petition route). A victim who submits a document citing repealed sections will be turned away. No existing tool has rebuilt its citation database for BNSS. This is a structural gap, not a polish issue.

Failure 2 — No tool handles the escalation chain as a conditional decision tree

The legal path is not a single letter. It is: (A) SP complaint if the station refuses → wait 3 days → (B) District Magistrate petition under S.175(3) BNSS if SP doesn't act → wait 15 days → (C) High Court writ if DM doesn't act. The correct document at each node depends on what happened at the previous node. No existing tool generates all three documents in sequence with the correct authority, correct deadline, and correct citation at each step. They generate one letter and stop.

Failure 3 — The input mode is wrong for this user

Every legal aid tool requires the victim to type their complaint in English or formal Hindi into a form. Sunita speaks, she doesn't type. She may have a handwritten refusal slip from the officer, or she may have nothing — just memory of what happened. The tool must accept: a photographed refusal slip (Gemma vision), or spoken Hindi narrative (STT), or both. Typed English form input is the wrong interface for 800 million Hindi-speaking Indians with low written literacy. No existing tool supports voice-first input grounded to legal citation output.

4
Core AI mechanism

The insight: Gemma 4 collapses three hard tasks into one on-device inference pass — understanding spoken or handwritten Hindi input describing a crime, classifying the offense type against a legal taxonomy to determine if the refusal was lawful, and then generating correctly cited BNSS documents targeted at the right authority. No other model can do all three offline on a ₹8,000 Android phone.

What makes this non-trivial is the offense classification step. "Mera paisa le gaya" could be theft (S.303 BNSS — cognizable, police must register) or a contractual dispute (non-cognizable — refusal is lawful). Gemma must understand the contextual difference: was there consent? Was it taken secretly? Was force involved? This requires language understanding, not keyword matching. A rule-based system will misclassify it; a cloud model won't work offline; a smaller model lacks the legal reasoning depth. Gemma 4 at 4B parameters, with a structured prompt injecting the BNSS cognizability table, handles this accurately. The offense classification determines the entire downstream document chain — if it is wrong, the output is harmful. Gemma's reasoning is verified by displaying the classification rationale on screen and requiring victim confirmation before documents are generated. The AI reasons; the human decides.

5
System pipeline
1

Input capture — dual modality

Victim either photographs the police refusal slip (if they received one) or presses the microphone button and speaks what happened in Hindi. Both paths are available simultaneously. If a refusal slip is photographed: OpenCV pre-processes (deskew, contrast, binarize). If voice: Android SpeechRecognizer offline model transcribes to Hindi text. Both outputs converge as a text string for Step 2.

on-devicefn: preprocess_image(img) → textfn: transcribe_voice() → text
2

Entity extraction — Gemma 4 pass 1

Structured prompt: extract station name, officer name (if given), date of refusal, and the victim's description of what happened. Output: JSON with five fields. This is a fast, constrained call — extraction only, no reasoning yet. If any field is missing, the app prompts the user with a specific question ("Kaunsa police station tha?") before proceeding. Partial extractions do not block the flow — station name is the only required field.

on-devicefn: extract_entities(text) → {station, officer, date, incident, state}
3

Offense classification — Gemma 4 pass 2 (the critical step)

System prompt injects the BNSS First Schedule (Schedule of offenses with cognizable/non-cognizable flag) — 847 entries, ~80KB, loaded from SQLite into context. Gemma classifies: (A) what offense occurred, (B) whether it is cognizable under BNSS Schedule 1, (C) whether the police refusal was therefore lawful or unlawful. Outputs the classification with a one-sentence plain Hindi rationale. Displayed on screen. Victim must tap "Haan, sahi hai" before anything is generated. Misclassification is caught here.

on-devicefn: load_bnss_schedule(state) → contextfn: classify_offense(incident_text, schedule) → {section, cognizable, rationale}
4

Authority routing — deterministic lookup

Not generated. Pure function: state × offense type → SP name, SP address, SP email, DM name, DM address, DM email, High Court name and cause list URL. SQLite table: 36 states × ~5 authority tiers = ~180 rows. Updated manually before submission. This step cannot hallucinate because it is not a model call — it is a database lookup. The authority contact details are what make the complaint deliverable, not just symbolic.

on-devicefn: route_authority(state, offense_tier) → {sp, dm, hc}
5

Document generation — Gemma 4 pass 3 (three documents)

Three template-injected generation calls, each with: victim entities (Step 2) + BNSS section (Step 3) + authority contact (Step 4) injected as structured variables. The narrative paragraph (describing what happened) is the only free-form generated text — approximately 80 words per document. Templates enforce correct formal Hindi structure, correct address format, correct prayer clause citing the right subsection. Documents: (A) SP Complaint under S.166 BNSS, (B) District Magistrate Petition under S.175(3) BNSS, (C) High Court writ shell under Article 226 Constitution. All three generated in one session. Documents B and C are pre-generated and saved — they become active when the time threshold passes.

on-devicefn: generate_sp_complaint(entities, section, sp_contact)fn: generate_dm_petition(entities, section, dm_contact)fn: generate_hc_writ(entities, section, hc_info)
6

Countdown tracker + delivery

App sets two local notifications: Day 3 ("SP deadline. Aaj magistrate complaint bhejne ka samay aa gaya hai.") and Day 18 ("DM deadline. High Court writ ready hai."). Victim sees a live countdown on the home screen. Documents are saved as PDFs locally and shareable directly to WhatsApp. No cloud, no login, no account — the phone is the filing cabinet.

6
Tech stack
Gemma variant
Gemma 3 4B (int4, Q4_K_M quantized) — 2.3GB on-device. Chosen over 12B because: fits in 3GB RAM headroom on the target device (Samsung A03, 3GB total), inference time 4–7s per call on Snapdragon 680 which is acceptable for document generation (not real-time chat), and the 4B handles Hindi legal reasoning accurately when the BNSS schedule is injected as context. The 1B is too weak for offense classification nuance. The 12B won't load on 3GB RAM.
Inference runtime
llama-cpp-python — runs Gemma GGUF on CPU/GPU via llama.cpp. Python bindings, works on Android via Termux for demo; production would use MediaPipe LLM Inference API or MNN. For hackathon: run on a laptop tethered to the demo phone via local Flask API — this is a legitimate demo architecture and should be disclosed to judges honestly.
Image processing
opencv-python — perspective correction, adaptive thresholding, binarization. Runs in <150ms. Used for refusal slip photo pre-processing before OCR/vision pass.
STT (voice input)
Android SpeechRecognizer offline model (Hindi, downloadable at install, ~25MB). For demo on laptop: faster-whisper small model with Hindi language pack — runs on CPU in 2s for a 15-second clip.
TTS (output)
Android TextToSpeech API with eSpeak-NG Hindi phoneme engine — built-in, zero extra download. For demo: pyttsx3 with Hindi voice, or gTTS cached responses.
Database
SQLite — three tables: (1) bnss_offenses: 847 rows, section number, offense description, cognizable flag, punishment. (2) authorities: 180 rows, state, tier, name, address, email, phone. (3) sessions: local case log, document status, deadline timestamps. Total DB size: ~400KB.
Document output
reportlab — generates Hindi PDF with Devanagari font (Noto Sans Devanagari, embedded). Documents are formatted as actual legal letters with correct heading, address block, subject line, body, prayer clause, and signature space.
Frontend (demo)
Streamlit — rapid Python UI for the hackathon demo. Camera input widget, audio recorder, real-time document preview. Not production UI — honest about this to judges. Production would be a Kivy or Flutter app.
Cloud used
Zero. No API calls, no Firebase, no auth. The app functions permanently without internet. The only optional cloud feature: emailing the generated PDF to an NGO partner for record-keeping — opt-in, not required.

Offline strategy

There is no "offline mode." There is only one mode. The app is designed from first principles for zero connectivity. Every inference call, every database lookup, every document generation, every PDF creation, every countdown notification runs locally. The phone is the server.

Requires internet — one time only
App APK install (~45MB)
Gemma 4B GGUF model (~2.3GB, on WiFi or pre-loaded SD card)
Hindi STT language pack (~25MB)
Noto Sans Devanagari font (~8MB, bundled in APK)
Works with zero connectivity — forever
Voice capture and transcription
Refusal slip photo processing
Offense classification and rationale
All three document generations
PDF creation and local save
Countdown timers and notifications

What degrades gracefully (non-blocking): WhatsApp share requires WhatsApp to be installed (usually is). Email delivery of PDF to NGO partner requires network — if unavailable, the PDF saves locally and the share prompt retries when connectivity returns. The optional "check if your SP email is current" feature requires a 1KB fetch to a static JSON file — fails silently if offline, shows a "verify manually" message. The victim's core experience — classification, document, countdown — is unaffected by any network state.

Hackathon build plan — 20 days
Days 1–4 — Database (the foundation)
Day 1: Parse BNSS Schedule 1 (PDF → Python) into SQLite. 847 offense rows. cognizable flag, section number, punishment. Validate 20 random entries by hand against the gazette.
Day 2: Build authorities table. 36 states, SP + DM + HC contacts. Source: official state govt websites. Automate where possible, manually verify UP, MH, RJ, TN, KA (the demo states).
Day 3: Write the three document templates in Hindi. ReportLab + Noto Devanagari. Manually validate one complete document against a real BNSS petition filed in a UP court (findable on eCourts).
Day 4: Classification prompt engineering. Write 30 test cases in Hindi (varying crime types, ambiguous descriptions). Target: 27/30 correct classification. Tune prompt until this passes.
Days 5–10 — Core inference loop
Day 5–6: Gemma 4B running via llama-cpp-python. Entity extraction working end-to-end on voice transcript. JSON output validated on 15 test inputs.
Day 7–8: Classification pipeline complete. BNSS schedule injected into context. Confirmation screen working. "Haan/Nahi" flow tested on 20 real Hindi crime descriptions.
Day 9–10: Document generation pipeline. Three documents from one session. Authority lookup wired. ReportLab PDF output with correct Devanagari rendering. Test on 5 end-to-end runs.
Days 11–15 — Voice + UI
Day 11–12: faster-whisper Hindi STT integrated. Test on 10 audio clips of different accents (UP, Bihar, Delhi). Accuracy target: intelligible on 8/10.
Day 13–14: Streamlit demo UI. Camera input for refusal slip photo. OpenCV preprocessing. End-to-end photo → document flow.
Day 15: Countdown timer + local notification stub. PDF save + WhatsApp share button. Airplane mode test — run full flow with all network interfaces disabled.

Single biggest failure risk: Hindi offense classification accuracy. If Gemma misclassifies a cognizable offense as non-cognizable (e.g., calls a theft case a civil dispute), the victim gets wrong advice. This is not just a demo failure — it is a harm risk. Mitigation: (1) always show the classification rationale on screen in plain Hindi and require explicit victim confirmation before generating any document, (2) add a disclaimer: "FirstReport does not provide legal advice. Verify with a lawyer or NALSA legal aid centre before submitting," (3) build a hard fallback: if the model outputs a confidence-indicating phrase like "main sure nahi hoon," the app routes to a "please contact your nearest NALSA legal aid centre" screen instead of generating a document. The classification must never silently fail — it must either succeed visibly or visibly refuse.

10
One wow feature
Officer Accountability Mirror

At the end of every session, FirstReport generates one additional document: a formal complaint against the specific police officer by name under Section 166 BNSS, which makes it an offense for a public servant to disobey the law to the injury of a private person. This is not part of the SP complaint — it is a separate, targeted accountability document naming the officer individually. The victim can hold it as leverage or submit it to the State Human Rights Commission alongside the SP complaint.

The "I didn't see that coming" moment: the victim learns, for the first time, that the officer who turned her away did not merely fail to help her — he committed a specific, named offense under Indian law, and there is a document with his name on it that says so. The power dynamic visible in Sunita's face when she understands this is the moment that wins the demo room.

Why it's buildable in the hackathon: It is a fourth ReportLab template. The entity extraction in Step 2 already captures the officer's name if spoken or visible on the refusal slip. The BNSS section (166) is fixed — no classification needed. The State Human Rights Commission address is already in the authorities SQLite table. This feature costs approximately 4 hours of development time on Day 14, after the core pipeline is complete. The only new logic: if officer name is unknown, generate the document addressed to "the duty officer on [date] at [station]" — still valid legally.
